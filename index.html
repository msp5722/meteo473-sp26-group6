Winter Energy Stress Index

Summary

Overview

The Winter Energy Stress Index (WESI) quantifies atmospheric conditions that increase heating demand and energy grid infrastructure stress during cold-season events.

Intended Audience

  • Utility companies
  • Emergency management teams
  • Energy planners
  • General public

Variables Used

10m Wind Gust 2m Temperature Precipitation Rate Precipitation Type Wind Chill

Data Selection

The Winter Energy Stress Index (WESI) was developed and tested using data from the late January 2026 winter storm, a significant cold-season event that impacted large portions of the eastern United States. This storm provided a wide range of atmospheric conditions, including strong winds, heavy snowfall, mixed precipitation, and periods of freezing rain, making it an ideal case study for evaluating both energy demand and infrastructure stress with respect to the electrical energy grid system.

Why This Storm?

  • Diverse Conditions: Included snow, wet snow, sleet, freezing rain, and rain, allowing multiple precipitation types to be analyzed.
  • Strong Winds: Wind gusts increased the risk of infrastructure damage and enhanced wind chill effects.
  • Cold Temperatures: Low temperatures combined with wind chill significantly increased heating demand.
  • Realistic Impacts: The storm created conditions representative of real-world energy stress scenarios.

How It Was Used

  • ECMWF model forecast data from this event was used to calculate index values across multiple forecast hours starting with data from before and after the event to capture low and high end scenarios.
  • The plots on the website were developed using the atmospheric conditions from this dataset.

Index Explained

Energy Demand Drivers

  • Wind Chill: Primary driver of perceived cold, increasing heating demand as values decrease.
  • 2m Temperature: Provides the baseline atmospheric condition used to calculate wind chill.
  • Interaction: Wind chill amplifies the effect of temperature on energy demand.

Infrastructure Damage Drivers

  • Precipitation Type: Largest contributor due to varying impacts on power lines and stress on trees and branches.
  • 10m Wind Gust: Higher gusts increase risk of tree damage and line failure.
  • Precipitation Rate: Higher rates lead to faster accumulation and increased structural stress.

Model Approach

  • Precipitation Type: Linear scaling based on severity (freezing rain highest impact followed by wet snow, freezing drizzle, snow, ice pellets, rain/snow mix, and rain with the lowest impact).
  • Wind Gust: Quadratic scaling to reflect rapidly increasing damage at higher speeds.
  • Precipitation Rate: Linear scaling from 0–1 in/hr based on accumulation impact.
  • Wind Chill: Linear scaling with lower temperatures relating to higher index values.
  • 2m Temperature: Linear scaling with same methodology as wind chill.

Explanation of Weights:

  • Precipitation Type (27.5%): Highest weight due to direct impact on infrastructure damage, especially freezing rain and wet snow.
  • Wind Chill (27.5%): Strongest influence on energy demand by representing perceived cold experienced by the public.
  • 10m Wind Gust (20%): Contributes to infrastructure damage through increased risk of tree and power line failure.
  • 2m Temperature (15%): Provides baseline conditions but has less direct impact than wind chill.
  • Precipitation Rate (10%): Influences accumulation speed but is secondary to precipitation type.

Winter Energy Stress Scale (0–10)

Hazard values Explained

0–2 Low Minimal weather and grid impacts
2–4 Moderate Cold exposure begins, minor grid demand increase
4–6 Elevated Noticeable wind chill and rising energy demand
6–8 High Hazardous conditions with elevated grid stress
8–10 Extreme Severe conditions with high infrastructure and energy strain

Interactive Viewer

We also analyzed high-end and low-end scenarios to evaluate how the index performs across different winter weather conditions. These cases focus on the Northeast region. The high-end case is based on a strong nor’easter in late February, which produced elevated stress values, particularly along coastal areas. In contrast, the low-end case represents a lighter snow event in mid-January, resulting in much lower index values. This comparison highlights how varying storm intensity influences both energy demand and infrastructure stress.

About Us

Matthew Pollard and Patrick Dela Rosa are third-year undergraduate students at the Pennsylvania State University. We are majoring in Meteorology and Atmospheric Science via the weather risk management option and minoring in Energy Business and Finance. We created this website as part of our METEO 473: Applications of Computers to Meteorology final project.

Our project focuses on developing a Winter Energy Stress Index based on a case study analysis on a recent winter storm to examine how different atmospheric variables influence wintertime energy demand and potential stress on energy infrastructure. We chose this topic because it combines our shared interests in weather forecasting, societal impacts due to severe winter weather, and financial energy markets. The weather is a significant factor on how daily society and the economy behave, so this project summarizes well how winter weather conditions influence the energy system and market.

Our index incorporates several meteorological variables, such as 2-meter temperature, wind chill, precipitation type, precipitation rate, and 10-meter wind gusts. These variables were selected because they play important roles in determining both public heating needs and the likelihood of winter weather disruptions such as icing on power lines, tree damage, and power outages. Our goal is to effectively interpret and communicate to the general public and energy utility companies how severe winter weather can induce stress towards both household heating demand and risks to the power grid.
